Pollinators & Pollination

Discover how bees and other animals help flowering plants make seeds.

Look closely: a flower visit can help connect two plants of the same species.
01 · POLLINATORS & POLLINATION

What is pollination?

Pollination happens when pollen moves from an anther to a stigma of a flower of the same species. Animals such as bees can carry pollen as they visit flowers.

A flower visitor is not always a pollinator. It must move pollen to the right place.

Pollination is the transfer of pollen — it is not the same as fertilisation.
02 · POLLINATORS & POLLINATION

Inside a flower

The anther makes pollen. The stigma is the surface that receives it. The style joins the stigma to the ovary, which contains ovules.

Petals can help attract animal visitors. Nectar is a sugary reward made by many flowers.

The anther and filament together form the stamen. The stigma, style and ovary form the carpel.
03 · POLLINATORS & POLLINATION

Pollen: tiny grains with a big job

Pollen grains carry the male reproductive cells of seed plants. They are made in the anthers and can be carried by animals or by wind.

Pollen grains are tiny. Their shapes and surfaces vary between plant species.

Pollen is not the same as nectar: pollen contains reproductive cells; nectar is food for visitors.
04 · POLLINATORS & POLLINATION

A bee is built for flower visits

A bee's branched body hairs help pollen stick. Some bees also carry packed pollen on special hairs on their hind legs.

A bee uses its antennae to sense its surroundings and its proboscis to drink nectar.

Pollen caught on a bee can be brushed onto the stigma of another flower.
05 · POLLINATORS & POLLINATION

How an insect pollinates a flower

A bee lands on a flower to collect nectar or pollen. Pollen from the anthers sticks to its body. At another flower of the same species, some pollen can rub onto the stigma.

The transfer between flowers is what makes the visit pollination.

Pollinators move pollen between flowers; they do not deliberately deliver it like a parcel.
06 · POLLINATORS & POLLINATION

More than bees

Butterflies, moths, hoverflies, beetles, some birds and some bats pollinate flowers in different parts of the world.

Different pollinators visit different flowers. Colour, scent, shape and the time a flower opens can all matter.

Hoverflies can look like bees or wasps, but they are flies and do not sting.
07 · POLLINATORS & POLLINATION

Wind can pollinate too

Not all flowers need animal visitors. Many grasses and cereal crops release light pollen into the air, where wind can carry it to feathery stigmas.

Wind-pollinated flowers often have small petals, exposed anthers and feathery stigmas.

Grass pollen can travel through the air, which is why it can trigger hay fever.
08 · POLLINATORS & POLLINATION

From pollination to a seed

After compatible pollen lands on a stigma, a pollen tube can grow down the style. A male cell travels to an ovule and fertilisation can take place.

After fertilisation, an ovule can develop into a seed. In flowering plants, the ovary often develops into a fruit.

Pollination comes before fertilisation. They are two different stages.
09 · POLLINATORS & POLLINATION

Why pollinators matter

Pollinators help many wild plants reproduce and support the habitats that other animals depend on. They also help produce many fruits, nuts and seeds that people eat.

Not every crop needs animal pollination, but many crops benefit from it or rely on it.

Pollination connects plants, wildlife, food and healthy ecosystems.
10 · POLLINATORS & POLLINATION

Make a pollinator-friendly place

Grow a variety of flowers that bloom at different times. Choose suitable native plants, leave some undisturbed ground or hollow stems for nesting, and provide a shallow source of water.

Avoid pesticides where possible and never disturb a nest. Ask an adult before changing a garden or outdoor space.

A small, safe patch of flowers can provide food and shelter through the seasons.
